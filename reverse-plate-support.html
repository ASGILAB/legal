What the app does

Most nutrition apps ask what you ate. Reverse Plate asks what you want to hit. You set five dials — calories, protein, carbohydrate, fat and fibre — and it suggests real dishes whose portions add up to those numbers, with the cooking steps included.

The dials move on their own. Is that a bug?

No, that is the point. Calories are not independent of the macros: energy is roughly four calories per gram of protein, four per gram of carbohydrate and nine per gram of fat. Only three of the four numbers can be free at once.

So when you drag one dial, the others adjust to keep the arithmetic true. Tap the pin beside a dial to hold it still, and the remaining dials absorb the change instead. Pin two, drag a third, and the fourth is fully determined — it will move by itself, because there is nothing else left for it to do.

A dial will not go any lower

The calorie dial stops at a floor, and fat has a minimum. These are deliberate and cannot be turned off. An app that cheerfully planned meals for any number you could drag to would not be a responsible thing to build.

You can choose between the two floors under Pantry. That setting asks which floor the dial should respect. It is not a question about you, and nothing is measured against it.

It says my plate is short on something

That is the app being honest. Some combinations of targets cannot be met by any real food — very high fibre alongside very high protein, for example. Rather than quietly showing you something that does not fit, Reverse Plate shows the closest dish it can build and tells you exactly how far off it is.

I do not want to see a particular food again

Open any dish, and tap the block icon next to an ingredient. It will never be suggested again. Everything you have blocked is listed under Pantry, and you can allow it back from there.

Can I add my own food, or scan a barcode?

No, and that is deliberate rather than unfinished. Reverse Plate is a planner, not a food diary — there is nothing to log, so there is nothing to search or scan. If you want to record what you ate, a tracking app will serve you better, and the two work perfectly well side by side.

Today's meals disappeared

Today clears at midnight, by design. Reverse Plate keeps no history: no charts, no streaks, no record of previous days. It is a planner's scratchpad, not a diary.

The free trial and the subscription

Reverse Plate is free for three days, with the whole app available — nothing is held back during the trial. After that it is a subscription.

The trial converts to a paid subscription automatically unless you cancel at least 24 hours before it ends. To cancel, open the iPhone Settings app, tap your name at the top, then Subscriptions, then Reverse Plate. Cancelling is handled entirely by Apple; the app cannot do it for you and cannot see your payment details.

If you have paid before and the app is not unlocked — after reinstalling, or on a new phone — tap Restore on the paywall.

Where the nutrition numbers come from

Food data comes from the United States Department of Agriculture's FoodData Central, which is in the public domain. It ships inside the app, so everything works with no internet connection.

The figures are for typical examples of each food. Real ingredients vary, so treat every number as a good estimate rather than a measurement of what is in front of you.

Not medical advice

Reverse Plate is a cooking and planning tool. It does not know anything about your health and does not give dietary or medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or are managing a specific dietary requirement, talk to a doctor or a dietitian rather than an app.
